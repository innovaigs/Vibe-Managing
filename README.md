# Vibe Managing

### Building the Lovable for Business Management

Doing business is hard.

Starting a business is one thing. Building one that survives, operates well, grows consistently, and eventually becomes a large company is something entirely different.

The data supports that distinction.

The Global Entrepreneurship Monitor's 2025/26 research describes what it calls a growing **"Survival Gap"**: entrepreneurial activity is high across the world, but too few young businesses successfully make the transition into established companies.

In the United States, Bureau of Labor Statistics data show that, depending on the cohort, roughly half to somewhat more than half of new businesses are still operating after five years.

And scale is rarer still. Historical U.S. Census data from 2012 found that only about 5% of U.S. firms reported $1 million or more in annual sales or receipts.

The exact percentages change depending on the country, industry, year, and definition of success. But the larger truth is difficult to dispute:

**Starting a business is far more common than building a durable, growing, well-managed one.**

And when you have actually operated a business, it is easy to understand why.

A business owner is expected to understand customers, sales, marketing, hiring, leadership, finance, cash flow, accounting, taxes, compliance, operations, customer service, pricing, technology, strategy, vendors, contracts, forecasting, and dozens of other things.

Each area has its own terminology.

Each has its own tools.

Each has its own rules.

Each has its own specialists.

And every decision affects something else.

A founder does not simply have to know what they want.

They have to know **how the machinery of business works well enough to turn what they want into coordinated action.**

That is the part I have become obsessed with.

## The software parallel

Building software used to have a very similar problem.

Imagine that I have an idea for an application.

Knowing what I want the application to do is not enough.

Traditionally, somebody still has to think through the architecture, design the interface, choose the technology stack, create the database, write the code, connect APIs, handle authentication, debug errors, test the application, configure infrastructure, deploy it, secure it, and maintain it.

The person with the idea either had to understand much of that themselves or find people who did.

Then AI began changing the interface.

Lovable is one of the clearest examples.

Lovable launched in November 2024 around a remarkably simple belief: people should be able to build software even if they do not know how to code.

Instead of beginning with code, the user begins with **intent**.

You describe what you want.

You have a conversation.

The system translates that intention into software.

The databases did not disappear.

The APIs did not disappear.

The frontend did not disappear.

Deployment did not disappear.

Security did not disappear.

The complexity is still there.

**The difference is that much more of the complexity is absorbed by the system instead of being imposed on the user.**

That distinction is fundamental.

Lovable did not make software engineering cease to exist.

It changed who had to understand the implementation details.

And the market responded.

Within eight months of launch, Lovable said it had passed $100 million in annual recurring revenue. Around its first anniversary it reported $200 million ARR. By February 2026, it had crossed $400 million ARR.

That made me ask a much more important question than, "Can AI write code?"

I started asking:

## What problem was Lovable actually solving?

The surface-level answer is coding.

I don't think that is the complete answer.

The deeper problem was the distance between **human intention and technical execution**.

A person knew what they wanted to create but could not translate that intention into all of the technical decisions and actions necessary to create it.

Lovable began collapsing that distance.

And that leads to the question behind this thesis:

# What if we could do the same thing for business management?

What if the founder didn't have to know which dashboard to open?

What if they didn't have to know which report to generate?

What if they didn't have to know which marketing tool to configure, which CRM workflow to create, which financial metric to calculate, which operational process to document, or which specialist to call first?

What if they could start with the thing they already understand:

**the outcome they want?**

"Help me increase revenue by 20% without increasing headcount."

"We're running out of cash. Figure out why and give me a plan."

"We need 30 new customers this month."

"Our customer churn is increasing. Find the problem."

"I want to hire five people. Determine what roles we actually need, what we can afford, and build the hiring plan."

"Launch this product."

"Prepare the company for fundraising."

"Show me everything in the business that needs my attention this week."

And instead of returning another generic answer, the system could understand the company, create the plan, coordinate the necessary tools and specialized agents, execute what it is authorized to execute, ask for approval where human judgment is required, monitor the results, and adapt.

That is what I mean by **Vibe Managing**.

## Managing through intent instead of interfaces

Vibe Managing is not simply putting a chatbot on top of a dashboard.

It is not another AI assistant that gives founders advice and leaves them with another list of things to do.

It is not an AI CEO pretending that human judgment no longer matters.

The thesis is much more specific:

**Vibe Managing is an operating model in which a founder manages the business primarily by expressing goals, constraints, questions, and decisions in natural language, while an intelligent system translates that intent into coordinated business execution.**

The founder operates at the level of intention.

The system handles an increasing amount of the implementation complexity underneath.

To accomplish that, the system would need to understand the company's goals, customers, employees, financial position, products, commitments, processes, historical decisions, metrics, tools, and current state.

It would need persistent business memory.

It would need to know what is happening across the company instead of waiting for the founder to explain everything again.

It would need to reason across functions because real businesses do not operate in departmental silos.

A marketing decision affects cash.

A hiring decision affects runway.

A pricing decision affects sales, margins, and positioning.

A customer-service problem may actually be a product problem.

A revenue problem may actually be a distribution problem.

A cash-flow problem may have nothing to do with profitability.

The system would therefore need more than intelligence.

It would need **context, tools, permissions, memory, orchestration, monitoring, and accountability.**

And most importantly, it would need to know the difference between something it can safely execute and something a human must approve.

If sending a follow-up email is reversible, the system might handle it autonomously.

If spending $100,000, signing a contract, firing an employee, filing a tax return, changing payroll, or making a legally consequential decision is not, the system should stop and ask.

The objective is not maximum autonomy.

**The objective is maximum useful autonomy with the appropriate level of control.**

## Is anyone already doing this?

Parts of it are already appearing.

Shopify is turning Sidekick into an increasingly conversational operating layer for merchants. It can understand store context, surface recommendations, interact with other applications, and help merchants take actions.

HubSpot is becoming an agentic customer platform.

Salesforce is building Agentforce to execute enterprise work.

Intuit has spent years combining accounting, payments, payroll, marketing, and other financial workflows around small businesses.

There are AI systems for sales.

There are AI systems for marketing.

There are AI systems for customer support.

There are AI systems for recruiting.

There are AI systems for accounting.

There are AI systems for legal work.

There are AI systems for analytics.

There are thousands of individual pieces.

What I do not yet see is the complete abstraction layer.

I do not see the equivalent of saying:

**"Here is my business. Here is where we are. Here is where I want to go. Help me run it."**

That is the territory I am interested in.

## Is it actually possible?

I don't know yet whether the entire vision can be solved with the technology available today.

I do know that far more of it is technically possible now than was possible even a few years ago.

AI models can reason across large amounts of information.

Agents can use software tools.

APIs can connect accounting systems, banks, CRMs, advertising platforms, payroll systems, support platforms, calendars, email, project management tools, and databases.

Models can analyze information, generate plans, execute multi-step workflows, monitor results, and escalate exceptions.

But there is an important lesson from what has happened in software development.

AI has made coding significantly more accessible, but it has not made correctness irrelevant.

Developers are using AI at extraordinary rates, yet research still shows significant distrust of AI-generated outputs.

That means the hardest problem in Vibe Managing may not ultimately be intelligence.

It may be **trust**.

Can the system understand the company accurately?

Can it use the correct data?

Can it know when it is uncertain?

Can it distinguish advice from action?

Can it operate inside strict permissions?

Can every important action be audited?

Can mistakes be reversed?

Can it recognize when a CPA, lawyer, HR professional, executive, or founder needs to intervene?

Can a founder trust it with increasingly consequential parts of the company?

Those are not side questions.

They may be the core product.

## Will businesses pay for this?

Businesses already spend enormous amounts of money attempting to solve pieces of the problem.

They pay for accounting software.

CRM.

Payroll.

Marketing platforms.

Project management.

Analytics.

Consultants.

Agencies.

Recruiters.

Bookkeepers.

Lawyers.

Fractional executives.

Operations employees.

And increasingly, AI agents.

Intuit's Global Business Solutions business generated more than $11 billion in fiscal 2025. HubSpot generated more than $3 billion serving businesses through its customer platform. Salesforce's Agentforce reached more than $1 billion in annual recurring revenue not long after launch.

That does not prove that businesses will pay for **Vibe Managing**.

That hypothesis still needs to be tested.

But it proves something important:

**Businesses already spend enormous amounts of money trying to manage complexity.**

The opportunity may not require creating an entirely new budget.

It may require creating a dramatically better way of converting existing business-management spending into outcomes.

## The real product thesis

The product should not begin as "AI that runs every company."

That would be too broad to build, too difficult to trust, and almost impossible to validate.

Lovable did not begin by automating every possible form of software engineering.

The path is to find the smallest version of Vibe Managing that produces an outcome so clearly better that people immediately understand the future hiding inside it.

Then expand.

One workflow.

Then several connected workflows.

One function.

Then several functions.

One business context.

Then a complete business model.

Over time, the interface between the founder and the company changes.

Today a founder manages a business by jumping among dashboards, meetings, spreadsheets, emails, employees, consultants, and dozens of SaaS applications.

My hypothesis is that in the future, much of that will collapse into a single intelligent operating layer.

The founder will communicate intent.

The system will coordinate execution.

And the underlying complexity will increasingly disappear from the founder's immediate experience in the same way infrastructure, databases, frameworks, and deployment are increasingly disappearing from the experience of the vibe coder.

## Why this matters

I do not believe business should require a person to become an expert in ten different professions before they have the right to build something meaningful.

That does not mean expertise has no value.

It means expertise should increasingly be embedded into the infrastructure.

People should still have to create value.

They should still have to understand customers.

They should still have to make difficult decisions.

They should still need judgment, creativity, ambition, resilience, and leadership.

AI should not remove the parts of entrepreneurship that make entrepreneurship meaningful.

It should remove unnecessary complexity surrounding them.

A person with a great idea should be able to spend more time understanding the problem they are solving and less time figuring out which of 37 dashboards contains the answer.

A small founder should eventually have access to operational intelligence that historically required an executive team.

A ten-person company should be able to operate with capabilities that once required one hundred people.

And people who know their customers and industries deeply should not be prevented from building great companies merely because they have not spent twenty years learning the mechanics of every business function.

That is the world I want to investigate.

## The question

So the question I am pursuing is no longer:

**Can AI help people manage businesses?**

We already know it can.

The question is:

**How much of the distance between entrepreneurial intent and business execution can we eliminate?**

Can managing a company eventually feel like a conversation?

Can the founder say what they want to accomplish and have an intelligent system figure out what needs to happen underneath?

Can we build the infrastructure that makes sophisticated business management accessible without removing human judgment?

Can we move from software that helps founders perform tasks to software that understands the business and helps accomplish outcomes?

I believe we can.

I don't yet have every answer.

I don't know how far the technology will allow us to go.

I don't know which part of the problem will prove hardest.

I don't know what the first perfect wedge will be.

And I don't know whether the final product will look exactly like the one I imagine today.

Those are questions to investigate, not facts to pretend I already know.

But I believe the problem is real.

I believe it is worth solving.

And enough of the underlying technology now exists that the hypothesis deserves to be tested seriously.

Lovable helped translate **human intent into software**.

The company I am imagining would translate **entrepreneurial intent into coordinated business action**.

That is Vibe Managing.

And that is the journey I am beginning.
